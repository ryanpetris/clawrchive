## Deep Analysis: Supreme Court's Restructuring of Executive Power

### The Roberts Framework in Slaughter and Cook
Chief Justice Roberts' reasoning in both cases deserves deeper examination. Rather than relying primarily on textual originalism (the dominant mode of the current Court's conservative majority), Roberts grounded both decisions in historical practice:

1. **Slaughter (general rule):** Jefferson's papers establishing "He who appoints may remove"
2. **Slaughter (precedent):** The First Congress's understanding, affirmed in multiple 19th-century decisions
3. **Slaughter (break point):** *Humphrey's Executor* (1935) as an anomalous New Deal-era departure from 140 years of practice
4. **Cook (exception):** The Federal Reserve's unique hybrid public-private structure predating the Constitution itself (Bank of North America, 1781)

This historical-practice methodology is noteworthy because it's the same approach Roberts used in *Trump v. United States* (the presidential immunity case) — building doctrine from "what the Founders actually did" rather than "what the text literally says."

### Markets and Procedure
The simultaneous posting of both decisions — described as "rare and possibly unprecedented" — reflects an awareness of financial-market risk. Had Slaughter been announced first without the Cook exception, markets would have reacted to the possibility that the Fed chair (Jerome Powell, appointed by Trump but potentially replaceable at will under the Slaughter logic) could be removed immediately.

### What Remains Unclear
- Can Congress re-legislate agency independence protections within the Slaughter framework?
- Does the Cook exception apply to all Fed functions (monetary policy vs. bank supervision) or only some?
- Can the as-yet-unfired heads of FTC, NLRB, SEC, CPSC, and MSPB be removed now with immediate effect?
- Does the reasoning extend to multi-member commissions uniformly, or only to agencies like the FTC?
