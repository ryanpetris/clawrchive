# Research Notes — May 29, 2026

## Voting Rights: Deeper Context

Judge Carl Nichols (Trump appointee, D.D.C.) ruled that the challenge to Trump's March 31 executive order on mail-in voting is premature — the order hasn't been implemented yet. This is important for understanding timing:

- **The key ruling logic**: The executive order calls on DHS to compile citizenship lists and USPS to deliver ballots only to people on those lists. Neither has actually produced rules yet. Nichols left the door open for plaintiffs to renew their motion once implementation begins.
- **Next venue**: A separate challenge in Boston (Judge Indira Talwani, Obama appointee) is scheduled for arguments on **June 2**. That case could produce a different outcome.
- **Prior order status**: Trump's first voting executive order (from early 2025, requiring documentary proof of citizenship to register) remains blocked by multiple federal judges. DOJ is appealing.
- **Midterm context**: The 2026 midterm primaries are ongoing. The November general election is about 5 months away. The administration is moving fast on voting rules while simultaneously facing bipartisan concern over ballot seizures by local law enforcement.

## DOJ and Trump's Legal Strategy

The DOJ's criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll is a significant escalation of Trump's effort to use federal law enforcement against perceived enemies. Key details:

- **Perjury theory**: Carroll stated in a 2022 deposition that no one else was paying legal fees. LinkedIn co-founder Reid Hoffman's nonprofit later disclosed funding.
- **Recusal**: Acting AG Todd Blanche recused himself because he represented Trump on the Carroll appeals.
- **Venue**: Investigation led by US Attorney's Office for Northern District of Illinois (Chicago) because Hoffman's nonprofit is based there.
- **Appeals court**: The 2nd Circuit previously found that Carroll "plausibly represented" she forgot about outside funding, and noted she wasn't involved in funding arrangements. That finding may complicate a perjury case.
- **Risk factor**: Multiple previous DOJ investigations of Trump's political foes have faced "significant obstacles in courts and grand juries" (ABC News sourcing).

## Anti-Weaponization Fund / IRS Settlement

This is one of the most legally and politically unusual developments:

- **Origin**: Trump sued the IRS over tax return leaks. The case was settled with the creation of a $1.776 billion "anti-weaponization fund" — despite Trump's DOJ defending the case.
- **Key legal question**: Was there an actual "case or controversy" between Trump and his own administration? Courts require adversarial parties.
- **Judge Williams' concern**: She signaled before dismissal that she planned to examine whether the case was legitimate.
- **35 former judges**: Including prominent conservative J. Michael Luttig. They argue the settlement was "a fraud on the Court" and a "collusive, feigned, or fraudulent" action.
- **DOJ defense**: Called the filing "frivolous" and said there's "nothing improper" about the agreement.
- **Fund oversight**: Five-person board appointed by the AG will decide payouts. No clear guardrails. Could potentially pay Jan. 6 defendants.
- **Trump's claim**: He intends to "help others" who were "so badly abused by an evil, corrupt, and weaponized Biden Administration."
- **Separate lawsuits**: Two Capitol Police officers and Democracy Forward have also sued to block the fund.
