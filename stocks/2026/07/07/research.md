# Research Notes — Tech Stock Briefing — July 7, 2026

## Deeper Analysis & Discarded Findings

### AI Infrastructure Capex — Is the ROI Real?

The $715B combined CapEx figure for 2026 is staggering. A few points worth noting beyond the headlines:

- **Amazon's $200B:** Much of this goes to Trainium chips and AWS data center expansion. Amazon explicitly missed Q1 operating income forecasts due to the spending, sending shares down 10%. This pattern may repeat in Q2.
- **Google's $185-190B:** TPU v7 is the big spend. Google's advantage: TPUs are used internally (search, YouTube, Gemini) so ROI is partially self-contained. External cloud revenue is a bonus.
- **Microsoft's $120B:** The Stargate JV (OpenAI/SoftBank/Oracle) adds $500B on top of this. Microsoft faces the highest scrutiny because Azure's exclusive OpenAI relationship ended in April 2026.
- **Meta's $125-145B:** The smallest absolute but highest relative to revenue. Llama 4 training costs are enormous. Meta has no cloud business to monetize — all spend is for internal products and ads.

**Key question for earnings:** Is any Big Tech company signaling a CapEx *slowdown* in H2? The bull case for semis depends on continued acceleration. If even one hyperscaler hints at digestion, the SOX pulls back hard.

Not enough to move AMD's revenue needle in the near term. The win is symbolically important (competition for NVDA's automotive pipeline) but not financially material for H2 2026.

The real story in semis right now is *supply discipline*, not *demand acceleration*:

- DRAM contract prices: +58-63% QoQ in Q2 2026
- NAND prices: +70-75% QoQ
- HBM: effectively sold out for all of 2026

The question is whether Samsung and SK Hynix maintain pricing discipline. Samsung's "sell the news" on a record quarter suggests the market fears they won't.

**Watch item:** If Samsung announces new capacity expansion plans, MU drops hard. If Samsung cuts capex, memory rally has room.

### The Strait of Hormuz — What Markets Are Pricing

Brent at $72.29 is remarkable given the circumstances. Pre-war Brent was ~$75-80. The war pushed it to $114. Now it's *below* pre-war levels despite ongoing attacks.

What the market is pricing:
1. The ceasefire framework holds broadly (even if individual attacks occur)
2. OPEC+ is adding supply (188K bpd from August)
3. Saudi cutting prices to Asia ($11 reduction in OSP — biggest in 20+ years)
4. U.S. production staying elevated

What the market is *not* pricing:
1. Full re-closure of the strait (this scenario would send Brent back above $100)
2. Iran tolls starting mid-August (disputed, could raise shipping costs)
3. LNG supply disruption (already seen a tanker strike)
